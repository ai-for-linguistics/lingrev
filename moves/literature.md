# Reviewing with literature

Use supplied papers and links as evidence, alongside the current manuscript. Accept PDFs, readable source, bibliography files, and links. Identify which sources are background, competing analyses, or sources of potentially relevant facts. Do not require the author to classify everything before starting if the role is clear.

1. Inventory what you actually read, including accessible sections of linked material. Record inaccessible sources and extraction problems. Bibliographic metadata alone is not a reading of the source.
2. Compare concrete claims, assumptions, definitions, predictions, evidence, and scope. Cite accurate locators in both the manuscript and comparison source. Distinguish an author claim from your interpretation.
3. Look for an existing fact or argument that challenges the account, a missing comparison, a useful alternative principle, or a new test. Reconstruct the theory's prediction under its own assumptions before declaring a contradiction.
4. Put specific issues and useful additions in Comments. For a candidate counterexample, record its provenance, alternative readings, and what would settle the issue. A remembered fact is a lead to verify, not inspected evidence. A limited source set cannot establish novelty.
5. Update Coverage & sources with what was read and what remains inaccessible. If more literature would help, make a bounded offer to extend the search, with a reason for each source.

Do not fabricate references, quotations, page numbers, or results. Web searches, where available and authorized, should leave a reproducible trail of sources inspected. Preserve the author's confidentiality and do not upload unpublished material to an external service just to search for comparisons.

A useful instruction in a comment could be: “Compare this claim with the supplied alternative account. Show the assumptions and predictions in both sources, identify the passages you inspected, and propose a discriminating example. Mark anything you could not check.”
